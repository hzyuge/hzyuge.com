Fly Fishing vs Spinning: Which One Should You Start With?

📅 2026-05-20 ⏱ 10 min read

When I tell people I fish, the first question is usually "what kind?" And when I say "both fly and spinning," I get a look like I've admitted to speaking two languages fluently. The truth is, they're completely different disciplines — like the difference between painting with watercolors and sketching with charcoal. Both create art. The approach, tools, and mindset are worlds apart.

If you're trying to decide which path to take as a beginner, I've got good news and bad news. Bad news: you'll eventually want to do both. Good news: one is definitely easier to start with. Let me break it down from real experience.

Spinning: Practical and Forgiving

Spinning gear is what most people picture when they think "fishing." A rod with line guides on the bottom, a reel hanging underneath, and a simple cast-and-retrieve motion. It's the Toyota Camry of fishing — reliable, accessible, and gets the job done without drama.

With a spinning setup and a decent rod, you can be on the water and fishing effectively within an hour of buying your gear.

Pros:

  • Easy to learn — the casting motion is intuitive
  • Works everywhere — ponds, lakes, rivers, surf
  • Handles wind well — heavier lures punch through gusts
  • Wide range of techniques — bottom fishing, trolling, jigging, topwater
  • Cheaper entry point — a solid spinning combo starts around $50-100

Cons:

  • Less delicate presentations — harder to present tiny dry flies
  • Line memory — monofilament can get twisty and tangled
  • Can feel "mechanical" — less of the artistry that fly fishing offers

Fly Fishing: Artful and Addictive

Fly fishing is a different beast entirely. You're casting the line, not the lure. The "fly" is nearly weightless — a tiny imitation of an insect made from fur and feathers. The casting motion is rhythmic, almost meditative: back cast, pause, forward cast, present. When it clicks, it's beautiful. When it doesn't, you'll spend more time untangling your leader than actually fishing.

Pros:

  • Incredibly precise presentations — drop a dry fly on a dinner plate at 40 feet
  • Deeply satisfying when you master it — the cast itself becomes the reward
  • Access to trout streams — many are fly-fishing only
  • Aesthetic and tradition — there's a craft and history to it

Cons:

  • Steep learning curve — expect weeks of frustration before you're competent
  • Weather-dependent — wind is your enemy
  • Limited to certain species and waters — not ideal for deep water or heavy cover
  • More expensive entry — a decent fly setup starts around $150-200

My Honest Recommendation for Beginners

Start with spinning. Learn to read water. Learn what fish eat. Learn to feel a strike and set the hook. Build confidence. Then pick up a fly rod if the bug bites you. Going straight to fly fishing is like learning to drive in a Formula 1 car — possible, but unnecessarily hard.

I started with a cheap spinning combo from a hardware store. Caught hundreds of fish. Then one day I saw a guy on a mountain stream, casting a fly line in perfect loops, the sun catching the mist, and I thought: I want to do that. So I did. But I was glad I'd already learned the fundamentals with a spinning rod.

Whichever path you choose, the fish don't care about your gear. They care about whether your presentation looks natural. Master that, and you'll catch fish with either setup.
